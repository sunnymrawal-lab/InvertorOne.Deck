About the platform

The future of healthcare has a perfect memory.

NVAROG is building a health-identity platform for India, founded on a simple idea: your health story should follow you, the way your phone number and your data already do. A generation ago, a telephone was tied to a wall - you walked to it. Mobile changed that forever, your voice and data now travel with you everywhere. Health records never made that leap. They still sit scattered across the world - a paper file in a cupboard at home, a lab report buried in email, a prescription photo on WhatsApp, an X-ray left behind in the last city you lived in. Every time a family moves, changes doctors, or faces an emergency, that history starts again from zero. NVAROG exists to end that - to give every person a single, verified, lifelong health record that travels with them, and one-touch consent to share their whole history with any hospital or doctor in seconds, and revoke it just as fast.

In practice, that promise shows up as three things working together: a single patient-owned health record, portable across hospitals, cities and countries, with one-touch consent to share or revoke access, a connected care layer - caregiver oversight, teleconsults, diagnostics, pharmacy and insurance - that plugs into that record as trust grows, and, built on top of both, a super app for a person's entire health life - one place to capture vitals, hold records, oversee a parent's care, consult a doctor and manage everything in between, for anyone in India to start building their own perfect memory.

The platform enters through the moment families feel this most acutely: caring for ageing parents - and it enters through the caregiver who is already there. Most families have already found someone to look after an ageing parent, what they don't have is visibility into that care. NVAROG digitizes the daily effort of the caregiver already in the house - vitals, medications, visits, notes - and turns it into a live view the family can watch from anywhere, giving them eyes and ears on care they couldn't otherwise see, especially from another city or country. But eldercare is the doorway, not the destination. Underneath the care service sits the real asset: a portable, patient-owned health record that belongs to the person, never the provider or the caregiver who logged it. As trust compounds, that record becomes the spine of a broader ecosystem - connected doctors, teleconsults, diagnostics and pharmacy, insurance, and a super app that lets anyone in India start capturing their vitals and building their own perfect memory.

What makes that record compound in value is a simple design principle: one record, many contributors. The same patient's file is read - and enriched - by everyone around her care. Her doctor feeds it a diagnosis, her pharmacy feeds it what was actually dispensed, her physiotherapist feeds it a recovery note, her caregiver feeds it a daily vitals log, her family reads all of it, from wherever they are, and adds context none of the others would know to ask for. Every contributor sees more because the last one wrote something down, and every new persona NVAROG onboards doesn't just add a user - it deepens the one record everyone else is already relying on.

What makes NVAROG distinctly Indian - and quietly global - is where it's built. It rides India's public digital health rails, ABDM and ABHA, the same way the last decade's breakout fintech apps were built on Aadhaar and UPI: the government lays the open infrastructure, and private companies build the products people actually touch. NVAROG is that consumer layer for health. Data stays in India, under the person's consent, on FHIR R4 standards - sovereign at home, yet engineered from day one to follow Indian families wherever life takes them, from Bengaluru to Boston to the Gulf. It is, in the end, a promise as much as a product: that the spirit of care can follow you everywhere, seeing you, not just your symptoms.

Every record on NVAROG stays owned by the person who lives it - nothing about it changes hands without their explicit, revocable consent. But with that consent, the same rails that let a family watch over a parent's care could let anonymised patterns from millions of records do something bigger: help researchers catch a dangerous drug reaction in months instead of years, the way real-world safety networks like the FDA's Sentinel Initiative already do, or point drug discovery at the specific biology of Indian patients, the way population studies like UK Biobank and FinnGen already do for their own countries. Nothing leaves a person's control without their yes. Consent can turn one record into someone else's cure.
